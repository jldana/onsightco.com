Four linked tools that move from the ground up: start with a budget to find your true annual draw, test whether semi-retiring around 51 holds, model the detailed bridge to Social Security, and tune the allocation glide that drives it all. Everything runs in today's dollars on real (after-inflation) return assumptions.

Build spending from the bottom up to land on your real draw — what the portfolio must cover after income, grossed up for the taxes a withdrawal triggers. That number is the input every projection below depends on.

Tip: type your Social Security estimate into the income section to see how much the draw falls once it starts — that's the difference between your bridge-years draw and your later draw.

The detailed version, with the part-time bridge, the college years (ages 54–57), and Social Security timing modeled explicitly. The first-year withdrawal stat is the early-warning light for sequence risk.

Over a 40-year horizon, a first-year withdrawal much above ~3.5% is leaning hard on good markets.

A Fidelity-style by-decade glide: each age band has its own stock / bond / cash mix that sets that band's return and volatility, plus a net flow. The bridge years (early 50s through Social Security) are where the allocation choice earns or loses the most.

For an early downshift, sitting at the conservative end of the 50s/60s ranges defuses sequence risk; let equity drift back up once Social Security carries part of the load.

This is a planning sketch, not financial advice. These tools organize and project assumptions you choose; they do not recommend a spending level, allocation, or retirement date. The output is only as sound as the inputs, and the return, volatility, and spending figures drive the result far more than the structure does.

All figures are in today's dollars and returns are real (after inflation), so spending stays constant rather than needing yearly inflation. Returns are drawn from a normal distribution, which understates real-world tail risk and single-stock risk; the models omit taxes beyond a simple gross-up, RMDs at 73, account-location effects, and Social Security survivor changes. A fee-only fiduciary — ideally one who does tax planning — can pressure-test all of it against your actual accounts.
